> Could you find your way in the dark? Could you find the exit of a maze with your eyes closed? What if you could find it in an instant?

I realize how absurd and illogical these questions may seem at first. Yet, in just three questions, I have summed up the subject I want to tell you about. What made it worth writing about was, so to speak, its “uncanny” side. The truly fascinating part, though, is that the answers lie beyond the rules of classical physics, in mechanisms governed by the surprising laws of the quantum world.

Today, if you are ready, we will talk about the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) complex, a remarkable structure found in green sulfur bacteria, organisms many of us remember from high school. It carries energy along unseen paths at extraordinary speed and with almost no loss, through a process in which quantum effects play a direct role and overturn our classical intuitions. Like something finding its way through a mysterious maze almost without error, this structure still astonishes scientists today.

## In the depths of the Black Sea

We do not have to travel very far to begin our journey. One of the places where the green sulfur bacteria that harbor this seemingly “magical” structure are commonly found is the Black Sea, off the northern coast of Türkiye. Its depths extend for kilometers, revealing what feels like another world as you descend. Life at the surface goes on as usual, but deeper down the light rapidly fades and water pressure rises many times over. Oxygen also quickly runs out, giving way to sulfur compounds. With barely any light, immense pressure, little oxygen, and abundant sulfur, the conditions transform life in the depths of the Black Sea. The organisms and ecosystems we encounter there look very different from those of the seas we know.

<figure class="fmo-figure fmo-medium"><img src="assets/fmo/deniz-beylab.webp" alt="Light fading from the surface of the Black Sea into its deep, dark layers." width="1200" height="920" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>

As you might imagine, surviving such harsh conditions is no small feat. This is where we witness life's dance with nature. The adaptations that allow organisms to endure these conditions are the result of millions of years of struggle. It would hardly be wrong to call them some of the most refined and functional structures we can find in nature. The FMO complexes we will examine today take part in the photosynthetic side of that dance, helping green sulfur bacteria photosynthesize deep in the Black Sea, in darkness, under high pressure, and amid sulfur-rich waters.

## The journey of light

To understand the structure and function of the FMO complex, we first need to look at the photosynthesis process in which it plays a part. As we know, photosynthesis works differently in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Green sulfur bacteria are prokaryotes, so we should set aside the chloroplast-based version of photosynthesis that most of us probably picture. Instead of such a complex system, these bacteria use a three-part arrangement that has always reminded me of a satellite dish. Let us take a look at it.

Let me explain the comparison. In the image above, the chlorosome is the dish, the FMO complex is, somewhat loosely, the route from antenna to receiver, and the type I reaction center is the receiver. Because I built this analogy around what each part does, it is fair to say that the chlorosome gathers light much as a dish collects radio waves, while the reaction center processes that light like a receiver processes the signal. Yet I could not quite fit the FMO complex, to me the most “magical” part of the system, into this picture. Its structure is unlike any comparable system we have managed to build with today's technology. If we need a tangible analogy, we could simply call it a cable—an exceptionally efficient one. It can deliver the energy gathered by the “dish” to the reaction center with an efficiency of around 99%. What is more, it does so while displaying quantum properties that, under these conditions, we would not expect to see.

But how? How can such a tiny, simple cell transfer energy better than we can amid all this chaos? The answer lies in those “quantum properties” I mentioned a moment ago.

## Superposition and coherence

Anyone interested in quantum physics will have heard of “superposition,” but let me briefly explain it for those who have not. Superposition describes something existing in several possible states at once. You might imagine, for example, an object being in both Ankara and Istanbul at the same time. When we observe it, however, we find it in only one location; the other possibilities disappear. We rarely encounter this in everyday life, but it is a natural behavior in the world of atoms and very small particles. Of course, superposition requires certain conditions. The system's quantum state must be preserved rather than disrupted by outside observation or interaction; this is called “coherence.” Low temperatures and minimal environmental effects therefore help superposition persist. If the system is disturbed from outside, superposition breaks down and the particle is observed in just one state. In short, isolation, low temperatures, and limited environmental influence all help sustain superposition.

So how can these bacteria maintain coherence deep in the sea, in an environment that seems to be the very opposite of the conditions coherence requires?

The effect discovered by Greg Engel and his team in 2008 sparked great curiosity in the scientific world. How could a simple bacterium achieve, deep beneath the sea, an effect that seemed possible only in a laboratory? The answer came from a very different approach, one that teaches us a great deal: if you cannot beat it, work with it.

## Proteins, pigments, and the environment

That is exactly what these structures do. They use the environmental effects we try to isolate to optimize energy transfer. To see how, look at the figures above. Because the FMO complex in Figure A is a trimer, you can picture it as three cables side by side. It may look tangled, but the energy actually travels through the bacteriochlorophylls shown in Figure B. The ribbon-like structures in Figure A are there for another reason—the reason behind much of this apparent magic.

Put simply, these proteins act like a gimbal for the pigments. Their natural vibrations hold pairs of pigments at favorable angles and positions for transfer. But wait: did we not say these systems work with environmental influences instead of fighting them? Exactly. Although the proteins help keep the pigments in useful positions, environmental interactions also set that positioning in motion and help correct it when energy transfer stalls.

## Finding a way out of the maze

Returning to our maze analogy, the protein structure is like the architect who designs the route to the exit: it lays out the paths and determines where each pigment belongs. But a traveler moving through the maze may encounter an unexpected obstacle. That is when environmental interactions come into play, helping the traveler find a new direction. The proteins provide the maze's overall plan, while the environment offers another way out when the traveler gets lost. Together, they allow energy to move through the bacterium's photosynthetic system as if a traveler lost in the dark were guided to the exit by a map drawn by the proteins and cues from the surroundings.

Do not imagine, though, that the proteins and the environment tell the energy, “Go this way” or “Take that passage.” It is closer to saying, “Try every way at once.” Imagine picking up a pen to solve a maze in a newspaper. Starting at the entrance, you try one path to the right, then another to the left. You work through each dead end until you finally reach the exit. If the FMO complex could solve the same maze, it would explore both the right and left paths at once. While you test each possibility one by one and spend all your energy finding the exit, FMO would seem to find the most effective route effortlessly.

Before the story carries us away and you start wondering whether FMO is somehow intelligent, let me be clear: there is no decision-making process here. For the energy being transferred, FMO is simply a system with an entrance and an exit—a maze that seems solved before anyone begins. It owes this ability to the quantum superposition made possible by the factors discussed earlier.

## From nature to the future

To sum up, the FMO complex and the way bacteria transfer energy using quantum properties challenge familiar assumptions in science and offer a new perspective. While we pursue isolation, low temperatures, and minimal environmental influence in our laboratories, bacteria transfer energy with extraordinary efficiency deep beneath the sea, in some of nature's harshest conditions, working in harmony with their surroundings. Recognizing that ideas such as quantum superposition are not confined to theory, but can also appear in practice throughout nature, broadens the horizons of human knowledge.

Discovering this remarkable arrangement could reshape our technological ambitions. Energy transfer systems inspired by nature, advances in computing and communications, and new steps toward unraveling the mysteries of quantum biology may all point to a more exciting future. Sometimes it is a bacterium, sometimes a protein, sometimes a pigment: each plays its part as effectively as it can, contributing to the system through a wider cooperation.

I want to close with one central point: the quantum effects associated with the FMO complex are significant to science, both as a source of inspiration from nature and as a step toward understanding quantum systems on larger scales. Quantum superposition and coherence observed in biological structures like these might have parallels in synaptic communication in the human brain or the navigational abilities of northern bald ibises. The observation of FMO in marine bacteria, in particular, suggests that quantum mechanical principles can persist outside the laboratory and within complex biological environments, opening a door to quantum biology on a larger scale. Watching nature closely is key not only to understanding its intricate mechanisms, but also to uncovering how quantum effects operate in living systems. Both basic science and future technology may find new horizons there.
